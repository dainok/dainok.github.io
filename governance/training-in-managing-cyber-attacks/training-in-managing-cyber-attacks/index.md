A cyber attack against a company can feel very similar to a terrorist attack. Shock, disbelief, fear and confusion are common emotions in the first hours after the incident is discovered.

Those first hours are also the most critical: this is when we must define the recovery strategies, and the ability to stay focused on the objective makes the difference.

## Acting on Instinct

In any traumatic event, panic reflexes often emerge. They are survival mechanisms deeply rooted in our biology:

* **Flee** from predators stronger than us.
* **Freeze** if there is no time to escape.
* **Fight** to protect our children or buy them time to run.

A cyber attack, however, is not a physical threat in the same room, and we cannot fight it with instinct. Recognizing panic signals and regaining clarity is essential to focus on the real goal: **restoring services and minimizing damage**.

Strong leadership plays a key role here. Leaders must reassure people, channel their energy and guide them toward the shared objective. Wasting energy fighting colleagues (fight) does not help: the threat is elsewhere and, in fact, it has already passed.

This is why many emergency plans, even well designed on paper, fail in reality: under pressure, rationality often gives way to instinct.

## Training as a Discipline

In my work, I emphasize **training** as a way to build habits: habits that help us react to attacks in a structured, fast and effective manner.

Training prepares people not only by defining procedures but also by **training the mind** to stay clear-headed when chaos takes over. Repetition conditions the brain to follow precise patterns, especially under stress.

This is not a new invention. I simply borrow from practices already used in the military and from the wisdom of martial arts.

## Training vs. Education

In cybersecurity, the difference between **education** and **training** is substantial:

* **Education** provides the theoretical and practical foundations to build and manage security.
* **Training** develops the mindset needed to react effectively when danger strikes.

Limiting training to technical staff is a mistake. True resilience involves the **entire organization**. Some examples:

* Training non-technical staff to recognize suspicious behavior and stop phishing attacks before they spread.
* Training security guards to spot weaknesses in their daily routines.
* Training top management to avoid exposing digital information that could compromise their reputation or physical safety.
* Training team leaders to manage crises with calm authority, guiding their people with confidence.

## Final Thoughts

The real outcome of training is not only handling a crisis effectively. It is building a team that is **cohesive, aware and resilient**, able to work together more effectively while contributing to the overall security posture of the company.

In the end, **cybersecurity is not just about technology. It is about people, and how prepared they are when the unexpected happens.**
