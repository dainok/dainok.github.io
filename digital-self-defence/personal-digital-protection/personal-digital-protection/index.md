As human beings, because of our evolutionary background, we struggle to perceive the dangers of the digital world. We have always assessed risk based on our physical environment, evaluating the context (location, people, etc.). Walking through a dark, deserted alley at night, for example, we would likely feel heightened alertness combined with anxiety. These emotions are essential to trigger a flight response in case of a threat.

This sense of vigilance is generally absent when we use computers and smartphones. Often this is because we use such devices in safe places, such as our homes, offices or crowded waiting rooms. Nobody would use a social network in a situation that is perceived as dangerous.

We fail to grasp fully, however, that if the Internet connects us to the world, the world is also connected to us, with all its consequences. This causes a misperception of danger when we use digital tools.

## Personal Digital Protection

We are used to thinking that certain individuals (politicians, industrial leaders) require physical protection through armed guards and armored vehicles. We rarely consider the need to protect these individuals from a digital perspective. This is not merely a matter of understanding a specific technology, but of adopting a security-oriented mindset.

Consider two well-known cases: [Boris Johnson](https://grahamcluley.com/uk-cabinet-zoom-meeting/) (former British Prime Minister) and [Ank Bijleveld](https://www.politico.eu/article/dutch-reporter-gatecrashes-eu-defense-ministers-videoconference/) (former Dutch Minister of Defense). Both inadvertently exposed access credentials to confidential meetings. Many colleagues commented harshly on these incidents, but the problem is more complex than it seems: we need to educate people to understand a world that, until recently, posed no significant threats. We must build awareness to mitigate the potential damage. I say "mitigate" because the damage has already occurred and, even if we start today, many more incidents will happen before enough informed people can minimize these risks.

We must focus on:

* C-level executives, politicians and key personnel in national and corporate environments, who are potential targets for attacks against critical infrastructure.
* Adults, both as individuals and as employees, who are susceptible to digital attacks, scams and phishing and can become entry points for corporate breaches.
* Children and teenagers, who are often given technological tools without adequate education on digital threats and self-protection.

## A Case Study

Several months ago, I came across a job listing from an international company (which I keep anonymous) looking for a CISO. The position was interesting, and before considering it seriously I wanted to assess the security awareness of the company. I conducted an OSINT (Open Source Intelligence) analysis to identify potential vulnerabilities.

For those unfamiliar with OSINT, it is the practice of gathering publicly available data, comparable to research in a public library.

I performed no hacking or intrusion, yet because of the careless handling of information, I made some surprising discoveries. Specifically, I found personal details of the CEO, including their private email, personal phone number and home address.

These details were accessible via the website of the company, embedded in corporate documents from previous years, probably submitted for certification purposes and never removed. Search engines then indexed this information, making it publicly searchable, just like a library catalog.

## Threat Modeling

Threat modeling means hypothesizing the threats to which I am exposed, or expose myself, by performing or not performing a specific action. For example, if I give my personal mobile phone number to someone who asks for it during a conference, I know that this number can potentially be disseminated without limit. As a result, I might receive WhatsApp messages from unknown people, and I will need to assess any interaction with this awareness.

In the scenario above, making one's private contact details public exposes the individual to digital attacks (via email and phone number) and physical attacks (via home address), potentially opening the door to stalkers. Since the individual holds a highly significant role in the company, they could also become the ideal target to access sensitive corporate information such as bank accounts, commercial data and patents.

## Conclusions

This article may seem alarmist, but denying reality does not make it disappear. The issue is real and widespread across all sectors (corporate, personal and youth). We must first acknowledge that we all face digital security challenges and learn to manage them effectively.

The tools exist: we need to learn how to use them correctly. The best way is to apply time-tested principles: we must train ourselves to recognize and mitigate digital threats, as we did with traditional security threats before the Internet became widely accessible.

The parents of today's middle-aged adults often advised against talking to strangers. We need to translate that mindset into the digital world, where we are all constantly exposed to strangers.
