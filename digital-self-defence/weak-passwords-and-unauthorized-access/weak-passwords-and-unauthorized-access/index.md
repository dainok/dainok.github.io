A few months ago, a new "collection" of data breaches began circulating: someone took the time to aggregate cleaned-up data from multiple past breaches into a single consolidated database. The result was a well-organized archive that contained more than a billion email addresses and the corresponding passwords.

This was nothing new, but its compact size and structured format made the dataset particularly interesting to analyze.

## We Have a Problem

While reviewing the file, it became clear that we face a significant issue: **many people still do not understand how to manage passwords, or why it matters.**

The analysis of a small but statistically significant portion of the database showed that **the chosen passwords were extremely weak.**

![[most-used-passwords.png]]

The problem is serious because **the same careless behavior that individuals have in their private lives often extends to the workplace**. This means that individuals expose themselves to risks such as identity theft, and **they also expose their companies to corporate security breaches.**

It is time to go back to the basics.

## How Attackers Think About Passwords

From the perspective of the attacker, the reasoning is straightforward:

1. Define an objective (often financial gain).
2. Assess the possible targets that can help achieve that objective.
3. Select the targets that optimize the effort: those that are most vulnerable or have the most available information.
4. Execute the attack.

With public online tools, past data breaches and marketplaces on the Darknet, attackers can identify potential victims easily. Exposed passwords and personal information give them a significant advantage.

## The Risks of Weak Passwords

By obtaining one or more passwords of a target, an attacker can infer patterns in the way the target thinks. The attacker can then try to access services with known or predictable credentials.

The risks are clear:

* For individuals: **account takeover and identity theft**.
* For organizations: **unauthorized access** to IT and telecommunication systems (as defined in article 615-ter of the Italian penal code).

This may sound like a distant threat, but the hyper-digital nature of today's world has led to an exponential increase in **account theft reports**. For organizations, this often shows up as **anomalous login activity.**

## How *Not* to Choose a Password

When creating passwords, many people believe they are being clever, but they often:

* Fail to realize how common their logic actually is.
* Underestimate the likelihood of being targeted.
* Overlook the consequences of a focused attack.
* Use trivial passwords (e.g., `margherita`).
* Use predictable keyboard sequences (e.g., `1q2w3e4r`).
* Apply "creative" formulas that they believe to be unbreakable (e.g., `googleLaVita3Bella`).
* Reuse the same password across multiple sites.

We must face reality: **every account that contains personal data has value.** The real question is *how much value*, and for email or social media accounts the answer is generally **a lot**.

From a corporate perspective, requiring users to set alphanumeric passwords of at least 12 characters, with uppercase, lowercase and special symbols, often results in passwords like `PizzaMargherita2012!`.

## Attacks Against Passwords

Let's return to the perspective of the attacker. Given the available information:

* If a target reuses the same password across services, a single data breach exposes **all their accounts**, including the corporate ones.
* If a target uses an algorithmic pattern, once one or two passwords are leaked, an attacker can deduce the formula and generate future credentials.
* If a password is simple, brute force attacks (with ordered probability dictionaries) can crack it quickly.

In nearly all cases, attackers can compromise at least some accounts.

## Choosing and Managing Passwords

Given the threat landscape, every account must be protected by a password that is:

* **Unique:** otherwise, one breach compromises everything.
* **Complex:** otherwise, brute force attacks succeed in minutes.
* **Non-mnemonic:** otherwise, attackers can reverse-engineer personal patterns from leaked credentials.

This leads to a practical challenge: how do we manage unique, complex and non-memorable passwords? The answer is password managers. Users need to remember only two things:

1. The master password of the manager's database.
2. The login credentials of their computer.

## Awareness and Training

This brings us to the main point: corporate security.

After an anomaly or a compromise, users are often asked to change their passwords. If the old password was `Cristina43!`, however, the new one will likely be `Cristina44!`, a useless change that lets the attacker keep access.

This is why training is crucial. Employees should not follow arbitrary rules they do not understand. They need awareness: they should recognize the personal benefits first, which then extend naturally to the company.

## Conclusions

I, too, once assumed that people understood the risks of weak passwords and how to manage them properly. Through conversations, I realized that the reality was very different. More importantly, I began to understand the motivations behind their poor choices.

By sitting down with people, discussing digital security and real-world consequences and, most importantly, listening, I was able to design a different strategy. This approach proved far more effective than generic "information security awareness" video courses.

The people I had the honor to train not only understood the risks, but also adopted a mindset that lets them think critically about the digital world. The benefits extended far beyond password security, reaching areas we had not even addressed yet.
