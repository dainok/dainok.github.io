I open this article with a quote from the Italian Data Protection Authority, which is also the title of a [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eLr5qFP68iQ "La protezione dei dati è un diritto di libertà"):

> Data protection is a fundamental right of freedom.

Let's reflect on the concept of **personal data**. Personal means something that belongs to us and whose ownership cannot be transferred. Under the GDPR, we can grant third parties the right to use our personal data, but at any time we retain the right to request its deletion from those to whom we granted that usage.

It is often said that:

> The vast amount of personal data that users pour onto the Internet every day is the new oil. {Andrew Keen}

Some services even suggest that we could profit directly from our personal data, instead of leaving all the gains to multinational corporations.

Let's analyze why "renting" out our personal data does not work, why it *must not* work, and why we should be more cautious.

## Why It Does Not Work

Consider Facebook, not to single it out, but simply to make the example concrete.

Facebook's revenue in 2019 was $16,886,000,000:

![[facebook-revenue.png]]

This is an enormous figure, clearly driven by the monetization of user data. The idea that users should share in this profit is appealing. There is a problem, though: the active user base of Facebook is enormous too.

![[facebook-monthly-users.png]]

Dividing the revenue by the number of active users shows that **the value of each individual user is less than \$10 per year**. Certainly not life-changing.

We might assume that providing *higher-quality data* would increase this value. For example, we could give Facebook our geolocation and smartwatch data, link all our accounts, export our browsing history and take part in every survey. Perhaps we would earn slightly more.

It still would not change our lives.

And, truthfully, Facebook would not be affected if we deleted our account or updated our profile. **We are just a drop in an ocean of 2 billion monthly active users.**

## Why It Must Not Work

Facebook states that it profiles users to improve their experience by:

* Showing news more aligned with their interests.
* Promoting products more relevant to their needs.

In reality, Facebook monetizes the ability to target audiences precisely with news and advertisements.

Now suppose users began to share more in the profits of Facebook. To pay us more, Facebook would need to generate greater value by offering new services. For example, if I were an influencer, I might promote a fashion brand suggested to me by Facebook, and Facebook could then sell this influence to major fashion companies.

The more unconsciously I adopt the suggestions of Facebook, the more effective they are. The more people I manage to influence, the more profitable I become. In short, Facebook could begin shaping my behavior, and that of my network, and resell this capacity as a new form of advertising. The more complete and higher-quality my data, the more powerful this influence becomes.

To increase user payouts, platforms must also increase their own revenue. They could not pay all users equally. They would instead trigger a competition in which users are incentivized to provide higher-quality data than others. This would let platforms reward a small subset of loyal users while gaining **greater power to manipulate the majority.**

## "I Can Quit Anytime"

Joining a social network is like smoking: many smokers claim they can quit anytime, yet they rarely do. And smoking leaves lasting traces in the body, which may resurface years later.

For many people, social media use is the same: a release valve into a parallel reality, curated with tailored content. We believe we are in control, exercising free will, yet day by day we are subjected to increasingly powerful manipulation.

True free will can be exercised only when we can resist the emotions triggered by posts selected specifically to distort our perception.

Science fiction? Hardly. Facebook has been known to manipulate, or assist in manipulating, public opinion by:

* Conducting social experiments that influenced the emotions of nearly 700,000 users ([Facebook apologises for psychological experiments on users](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2014/jul/02/facebook-apologises-psychological-experiments-on-users)).
* Interfering in political processes such as Brexit and the 2016 U.S. election ([The Great Hack](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iX8GxLP1FHo)).

Years later, little has been done.

Even if we delete our accounts, Facebook erases our data, posts and activities, but **it never erases the models derived from that data.** The GDPR protects personal data, but not the derived insights. The behavioral models, built on our preferences and actions, remain.

Moreover, research has shown ([NYT report](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/07/23/health/data-privacy-protection.html "Your Data Were 'Anonymized'? These Scientists Can Still Identify You")) that even a few simple attributes can re-identify individuals with 99.98% accuracy.

## Why All This Is Allowed

As stated earlier, data is the new oil. The reason should now be clear, but there is another dimension: large platforms are potential instruments of social control. Governments, particularly the U.S. government (most platforms are based there), are eager to exploit such powerful tools under the pretext of ensuring security.

[Clearview.ai](https://clearview.ai/ "Clearview.ai") is a company that scraped billions of photos from social networks to build a massive database. When we submit a photo to Clearview, the system, through [facial recognition](https://web.archive.org/web/20200207115721/https://www.nytimes.com/2020/01/18/technology/clearview-privacy-facial-recognition.html "The Secretive Company That Might End Privacy as We Know It"), returns all the matching photos of that person along with their sources.

Unsurprisingly, the first clients of Clearview include U.S. government agencies such as the FBI and DHS.

When it comes to privacy, many people casually claim to **"have nothing to hide."** Yet no one would be comfortable being part of a massive global mugshot database.

Let's be clear: Clearview\.ai is illegal because it **stole** user photos entrusted to social networks. In Europe, it also violates multiple GDPR provisions.

Yet Clearview\.ai remains operational and serves government entities worldwide, including in Europe. Remember the strict filters used to censor websites with copyrighted content? Clearly, there is no interest in blocking the services of Clearview.

## Conclusions

Renting out our personal data for money is equivalent to prostitution: we sell far more than just "data", and we receive only a few coins in return. We must approach the use of any digital tool or service with full awareness of the mechanisms at play:

> We must treat personal digital data with the same care and respect as weapons-grade plutonium. It is dangerous, long-lasting, and once leaked, it can never be recovered. {Cory Doctorow}
