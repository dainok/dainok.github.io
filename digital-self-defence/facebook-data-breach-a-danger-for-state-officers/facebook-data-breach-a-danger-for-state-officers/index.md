A few days ago the news broke about a [Facebook data breach](https://www.businessinsider.com/stolen-data-of-533-million-facebook-users-leaked-online-2021-4?IR=T "533 million Facebook users' phone numbers and personal data have been leaked online"): 553 million user records exposed in 2019 are now circulating freely online. The story caused an uproar because the dataset, which used to be sold for money, is now accessible to anyone for free.

Over 36 million of the records belong to Italian users and include phone numbers, Facebook IDs, dates of birth, employment details... **The dataset contains executives, MPs, senators, military personnel, law enforcement officers...**

The breach dates back to 2019, but the fact that it is now public changes the threat: a new category of **threat actor** has effectively been added.

## Threats to Individuals

Regular readers will understand why such a data leak is dangerous for people. Possessing or using the dataset is obviously illegal, but saying so does not stop attackers, stalkers, scammers, unscrupulous call centers or criminal organizations: they now have **a convenient phone directory for a large portion of the Italian population**.

## Threats to the State

The existence of such a dataset is not new, but it is worth reflecting on how it could be used to threaten the State. The database contains **a huge number of people who perform, directly or indirectly, critical functions for the State** and who have **potential physical access to highly sensitive areas**.

Most of these people are **not remotely prepared** to recognize and handle a digital attack. In a somewhat paranoid but realistic scenario that experts in my field consider, we can imagine **a massive, coordinated attack targeting these key figures**. Because it would be massive, such an attack could be unprecedented and likely highly effective.

## Countermeasures

As with any data breach, once the data is out there, the containment options are limited. Changing your phone number (the most sensitive exposed field here) is possible, but some data, like the date of birth, cannot be changed.

We must also accept that this type of criminal (misleadingly called a "hacker") is often a professional seeking to monetize skills, and sometimes even backed by intelligence services. With that in mind, we need to reassess seriously what personal data we publish online: **once a piece of data is online, it can become public**, and we must plan accordingly.

There are strategies to reduce our exposure substantially. For example, instead of giving our mobile number to every service (often touted as a way to "improve security"), we should evaluate whether the trade-off is worthwhile given the real risks. This incident shows that it may not be.

## Conclusions

This story is far from over. In my view, the original impact was underestimated when the breach occurred, and it is still being underestimated now. It is hard to know how those who bought the data used it over the past year. It is far easier, and far more concerning, to imagine how it will be used now that it is public.
